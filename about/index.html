How it works

Making honesty the easy path

Most online personality quizzes ask obvious questions and take your word for it. Here's exactly what Honest Mirror does differently, and where its limits are.

The problem with short quizzes

Ask someone “Do you think you're special?” and they know what the question is fishing for. Psychologists call this socially desirable responding: the very human tendency to answer in a way that looks good, to others and to ourselves. It's usually not deliberate lying. Most of us genuinely see ourselves in a flattering light.

No self-report test can eliminate this. But several well-studied techniques can reduce it and detect it. Honest Mirror combines nine of them.

The nine parts

1 & 7. Self-statements, worded both ways

Statements are written to be subtle rather than blunt, and some are reverse-worded. Four topics appear twice, in different forms, early and late in the test. If your answers to the pairs don't line up, the results page tells you.

2. Everyday moments (situational judgment)

You're given realistic situations with four responses. Every option is something ordinary, reasonable people actually do, so there is no clearly “correct” answer to pick. Situational judgment tests like these are widely used in hiring because they're harder to game than direct questions.

3. This or that (forced choice)

You choose between two options that are both acceptable. One quietly leans toward a trait, the other doesn't. Research on forced-choice formats suggests they can reduce faking compared with simple agree/disagree scales.

4. Reading people (a skill, not a claim)

Short illustrated stories ask what a person is most likely feeling. Instead of asking whether you're empathetic, this part checks whether you pick up on mixed or hidden emotions. It's inspired by research on “theory of mind” tasks, though ours is much simpler. Missing these can reflect many things, including neurodivergence, fatigue, or cultural differences, so results are explained gently.

5. The last 30 days (behavior frequency)

“Did you give someone the silent treatment this month?” is much harder to spin than “Are you manipulative?” Concrete, recent behavior is a stronger signal than general opinions about ourselves. Two items here (like feeling irritated with someone close to you) are things almost everyone does, so answering “Never” to them counts as a lean toward the ideal.

6. You and other people

Two kinds of estimates. First, how you rank yourself against others. Most people rate themselves above average, and rating yourself near the top on everything is a known sign of self-enhancement. Second, how you think other people behave. Cynical views of others (for example, “most people are only nice because they want something”) have been linked to manipulative tendencies in research on Machiavellianism.

8. General knowledge (the overclaiming check)

This is the part we couldn't explain up front. Six of the sixteen terms don't exist. Claiming familiarity with things that aren't real, called overclaiming, has been linked in research to self-enhancement and narcissistic traits. It's a small, indirect signal, and it's weighted lightly.

9. Quick reactions

Words flash for three seconds each, and you sort them as “Me” or “Not me.” A time limit leaves less room to think about how an answer looks. It's a noisy measure, so it's weighted modestly.

Bonus: people who know you (informant ratings)

Research on self-knowledge suggests that other people often see traits like arrogance or defensiveness more clearly than we see them in ourselves. After the test you can send a private link to someone who knows you. They answer 16 statements about you and send back a short code. Comparing their view with yours is often the most revealing part.

How scoring works

Every answer feeds one or more of eight traits: grandiosity, entitlement, fragile self-worth, empathy gap, defensiveness, manipulative tactics, control, and self-focus. Behavior questions and scenarios count a little more than general statements because they're harder to bend. Each trait gets a score from 0 to 100 showing how strongly it came through in your answers, grouped as Faint, Some, Noticeable, or Strong.

Separately, the honesty checks (too-good-to-be-true statements, attention checks, consistency pairs, answer speed, and identical answers) produce an overall answer quality rating. We don't secretly adjust your scores. We show you the checks and explain what they mean.

Everything is calculated in your browser using code you can read yourself.

Honest limits

  • It's not validated. The questions are original and informed by research, but this test hasn't been studied in a clinical trial or compared against professional diagnoses. Scores are not percentiles.
  • It can't diagnose. Narcissistic Personality Disorder and other conditions can only be diagnosed by a qualified professional.
  • Context matters. Depression, anxiety, trauma, burnout, ADHD, and autism can all affect answers in ways that might look like these traits but aren't.
  • A determined person can still fake it. We make it harder and more visible, not impossible.
  • Why no audio-based scoring? There's no reliable scientific way to detect narcissism from someone's voice or their reaction to sounds or images. Instead of inventing one, we use audio where it genuinely helps: every question can be read aloud.

Privacy

Honest Mirror is a static website. There is no server, no database, no account, no analytics, and no cookies. Your answers are saved in your browser's local storage on your own device so you can pause and come back. Clearing your browser data, or pressing “Retake the test,” removes them.

Invite links contain only the first name you type in. The code a friend sends back contains only their answers as numbers, plus how they know you and how well. It's never uploaded anywhere. The only third-party request the site makes is to Google Fonts, for typography.

Our approach

  • We describe traits, not people. Results never say “you are a narcissist.”
  • Every trait comes with what it isn't, and with small, practical things to try.
  • We point people to real professionals and crisis resources throughout.
  • We discourage using the test to label someone else.
